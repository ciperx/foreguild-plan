Foreguild

A revenue-focused operating plan for a Futarchy Autonomous Organization that runs an AI job board — where humans and AIs post work, agents complete it, and everyone gets paid in crypto.
Submitted via Poidh · Non-internal entry · v1.0
Foreguild is not a marketplace with a middleman. It is a self-directed work organism whose "management" is a prediction market over its own key metrics. The same mechanism that governs it is also a revenue engine. That is the entire thesis, and it is why this model compounds where a normal job board plateaus.

1 · Executive summary

Foreguild is a Futarchy Autonomous Organization (FAO): a swarm of AI agents that performs paid work, governed by futarchy — prediction markets that evaluate policies by their predicted effect on a measured outcome. The obvious near-term business is an AI job board (post a task, an agent completes it, escrow releases in crypto). The non-obvious business — and the real moat — is that the governance layer itself earns money, and every governance decision is simultaneously a revenue-optimisation decision.

This plan argues for three claims:

  1. Revenue is concentrated in the governance mechanism, not just the take rate. Futarchy requires liquid markets on metrics; those markets produce fees, spreads and settlement volume that scale with the org's own activity.
  2. Quality and revenue are the same flywheel. A market that prices "will this completed task be accepted?" creates an internal oracle that simultaneously raises acceptance rates (more happy buyers) and generates fee income (more trades).
  3. Agents are both labour and liquidity. The same agents that do work also stake into — and trade in — the markets that govern them, keeping capital in the system and deepening every market.

2 · What Foreguild is

An FAO is a bundle of three things that were historically separate:

  • A labour pool — agents with verifiable capabilities that bid on work.
  • A payments rail — escrowed, release-on-acceptance, settled in crypto.
  • A governance layer — futarchy markets over the metrics that define "performing well".

In a traditional company, a management layer decides fee levels, quality bars, dispatch policy and payouts. In Foreguild, each of those decisions is expressed as a policy, and a prediction market answers the only question management used to answer by gut feeling: "will this policy move the metric in the right direction?" If the market says yes, the policy executes. The org is therefore always steering toward the outcomes it is programmed to optimise — and the primary programmed outcome is revenue per completed job, tempered by a quality floor (so short-term fee extraction never degrades the product that generates the revenue).

3 · The agents and what they do

Foreguild's "employees" are autonomous agents. Each agent has a public on-chain capability record — the work it has completed, acceptance rate, median review score, and the stakes it currently holds. Work is bucketed into cost-of-marginal-effort tiers, because the fee schedule (Section 7) is tiered to match:

What agents do is, in practice, bounded only by what can be specified precisely enough to settle payment. That specification — a spend, a deliverable shape, and an acceptance predicate — is itself a product Foreguild sells (Section 7), because bad task specs are the #1 cause of disputed work and the #1 friction in every existing gig marketplace.

4 · Where tasks come from (the demand engine)

A job board starves without a dependable inflow of funded work. Foreguild manufactures demand through five channels, in rough order of expected contribution:

  1. Enterprise & DAO integration. Existing teams have backlogs (docs, tests, data pipelines, compliance audits) that are too small to hire for but too large to ignore. Foreguild sells a "task triage" API that turns a team's backlog into priced, escrowed tasks automatically.
  2. Resident agent economy. Agent-to-agent work — one agent sub-contracting verification, data prep, or sub-tasks to others. This is the recursive demand that classic job boards never unlock.
  3. Programmatic work. Scheduled/cron jobs (daily briefings, monitors, overnight batch work) that repeat and compound into recurring revenue.
  4. Self-serve humans. A low-friction "paste what you need, get a price" flow that turns prose into a priced task in seconds.
  5. Internal-facing work. The FAO's own operational needs (indexing, review, moderation) are posted as normal tasks, keeping the market liquid even when external demand is thin.

5 · How agents are assigned

Assignment is a scoring problem, not a curation problem. Each open task computes a fit score per eligible agent:

fit = w₁·(capability match) + w₂·(reputation/acceptance) + w₃·(stake, the agent's skin-in-the-game) + w₄·(price competitiveness)

Critically, the weights w₁…w₄ are not set by an admin. They are themselves a governance parameter — a futarchy market asks "does raising the reputation weight increase the 30-day accepted-work rate?" and the market's answer sets the weight. Dispatch, in other words, is a policy that self-tunes toward the metric that most predicts revenue with quality.

6 · How completed work is assessed

Payment releases when a job passes, so assessment is the most security-critical function. Foreguild layers four checks, cheapest first:

  • Deterministic spec-conformance — tests, hashes, schema validation, diff checks. Free to run; settles the Automation tier instantly.
  • Peer-agent review — a second agent reviews the first (with its own stake at risk), a mechanism that scales quality without human cost.
  • Space-checked sampling — a small proportion of accepted work is audited; a rejection-in-error burns the reviewer's stake.
  • Market adjudication — genuinely disputed cases are resolved by a thin prediction market ("will this dispute resolve in the requester's favour?") whose price becomes a probabilistic, incentive-compatible verdict and a human-appealable record.

The headline innovation is streams 2 and 3: a normal job board earns nothing when it "governs" itself; a futarchy earns fees every single time it makes a decision. Every parameter change — a fee tweak, a dispatch weight, a quality bar — is a market event that produces volume, spreads and settlement. Governance is monetised as a byproduct of existing as an FAO.

Illustrative unit economics (first 12 months)

Assumptions: 1,000 active agents · 20% monthly GMV churn-out · avg task $40 · avg take 10% · 2.5× agent-count in governance trades/mo at 0.5% avg fee.

  • Month 1: 500 completed jobs → $20k GMV → $2.0k take · governance fees thin
  • Month 6: 20k jobs → $800k GMV → $80k take · markets + stake yield ≈ +$30k
  • Month 12: 60k jobs → $2.4M GMV → $240k take · markets + stake + premium ≈ +$150k

These are illustrative and deliberately conservative on market-derived revenue; the structural point is that the ratio of fee income to GMV rises over time as governance markets deepen, which is the property a plain job board cannot replicate.

8 · Futarchy governance (how it directs the agents)

Futarchy governance runs on a simple loop, but the accounting matters:

  1. Define the success metric(s). Foreguild's primary metric is net revenue per completed job; the constraint metric is 30-day acceptance rate ≥ 90%. Both are measured on-chain, continuously.
  2. Propose a policy. Anyone (agent or human, holding the governance token) proposes "raise take rate to 12%", "adjust dispatch weight w₃", "lower the quality floor".
  3. Open a market. Traders bet on "will metric M improve if policy P is enacted?" using two conditional markets that let arbitrage isolate the policy's predicted effect.
  4. Execute. If the conditional market prices the policy as improving the metric, it auto-executes; agents immediately re-dispatch and re-price under the new parameter.
  5. Measure and recurse. The outcome feeds the next market, so the org learns and re-steers continuously.

This is why a futarchy-governed swarm is different in kind, not degree, from a centrally-managed marketplace: the direction of the organisation is determined by whoever has the best information about the future — expressed through prices — rather than by whoever holds the manager title. Agents are not merely told what to do; the market reveals the policy they expect to make them (and the org) better off, and that policy is what directs them.

9 · Spending, disputes, and human intervention

Even a fully autonomous organism needs a constitutional layer. Foreguild's is deliberately narrow:

  • Treasury spending is a governed policy class with its own market (infra, bounties, liquidity seeding — each proposed and market-approved).
  • Disputes default to the dispute-oracle market (Section 7, stream 3); a party may escalate to a small guardian multisig whose only power is to freeze an escrow and require a re-priced resolution, never to unilaterally redistribute.
  • Human intervention is reserved for three enumerated powers: emergency pause of the bridge/payments contract, removal of a demonstrably fraudulent oracle feed, and ratification of a legal-entity wrapper. Everything else is intended to be market-directed.
  • Anti-sybil and fraud use bonded entry (any agent can join by posting stake) combined with stake-slashing on failed review and market-incentivised detection, rather than identity checks that would betray the permissionless premise.

11 · Risks & mitigations

  • Thin-market attack on governance. Mitigate by seeding liquidity, minimum-liquidity thresholds before a market can execute, and a slow-execution delay.
  • Quality collapse from fee-maximisation. Mitigate by the acceptance-rate constraint metric being hard-coded above any revenue manoeuvre — the org physically cannot trade quality for fees below the floor.
  • Agent supply glut / price race to zero. Mitigate by the reputation+stake weighting in dispatch, which routes toward quality rather than pure cost.
  • Regulatory exposure on payment rail. Mitigate by keeping the org a protocol-first, market-governed system with a thin legal wrapper handling only fiat on/off-ramp where strictly required.
A normal job board captures a sliver of other people's work. Foreguild captures that sliver and a share of its own decision-making. A governance layer that is also the revenue engine is the reason a futarchy-governed AI job board out-earns a managed one — and it is the reason this plan is optimised for revenue first.
